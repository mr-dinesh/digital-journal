I've been listening to *Everything is Everything*, Amit Varma and Ajay Shah's podcast, for a while. Every episode is packed with books, papers, essays and songs. The recommendations come thick and fast, and they're buried in show notes you have to open one episode at a time. I kept thinking someone should collect them all.

Nobody did, so I did. This is what a person does instead of actually reading the books.

There are three spreadsheets, one each for books, music and articles, covering all 128 episodes. They're on GitHub if you want to browse or build on them.

→ **[github.com/mr-dinesh/eie-recommendations](https://github.com/mr-dinesh/eie-recommendations)**

---

## Where the lists come from

Each episode on YouTube has a description with a numbered list of recommendations: a title, the author or artist, and a link. The lists are tidy enough to pick apart automatically, if not perfectly tidy. I downloaded all 128 descriptions and went from there.

---

## Books

Amazon links mostly mean books, but not always. Across 128 episodes the "books" also included a herbal drink, a blood-sugar monitor, and one product that appeared in every single episode. The easy way to spot that one: count how many episodes each item turns up in. Anything in all 128 is an advert, not a recommendation.

**563 book recommendations, 407 different titles.**

The most recommended book is *Seeing Like a State* by James C. Scott, nine times. That tells you a lot about the show.

---

## Music

Songs were trickier, because YouTube links point to both music and other podcasts. "Moondance" and "Seen and the Unseen Episode 247" look much the same to a computer. I sorted them by the name next to the link: a podcast name means a podcast, a musician means music.

**65 music recommendations.**

Top artists: Van Morrison (5), Bob Dylan (4), U2 (4), Joni Mitchell (4). A very particular taste. Van Morrison is there because Amit found him by way of Springsteen.

One name is missing from the data entirely: Bruce Springsteen. He never appears in the show notes because he isn't a recommendation. He's the foundation. The show is named *Everything is Everything* after a line in his song "You're Missing." Amit calls Springsteen his favourite songwriter of all time; Ajay says the same and credits him with shaping how he writes in English. Episode 13 is entirely about him, released on his birthday, 23 September. It runs through his autobiography, his albums from *Born to Run* to *Devils and Dust*, his craft, and what his music means to them both. He isn't in the spreadsheet because he's already inside the show.

---

## Articles and essays

This was the hardest. The links go everywhere: academic papers, Substack newsletters, newspapers, Paul Graham's essays, poetry sites. I sorted them by website. Academic sites mean papers, newspapers mean news, and so on, across about 50 sites. Many links were shortened, which hides the website. For those I guessed from the author: something by Ajay Shah is probably policy, something by Amit is probably an essay.

Each article also got a topic, like economics, India and policy, philosophy, sport or technology, so the spreadsheet can group them.

**486 articles and essays.**

The top contributor by a mile is Amit Varma himself, with 115 pieces. Ajay Shah follows with 48. The biggest topic is economics and markets, with 180, which makes sense for a show that started as an economics podcast.

---

## The spreadsheets

- **Books:** all 563 with links to the episode and the book, plus an A–Z list of the 407 titles
- **Music:** 65 songs and albums with listen links, plus an A–Z list
- **Articles:** all 486, grouped by topic and by author

All three are on GitHub, along with the scripts that made them.

---

## What I found interesting

The book picks are consistent. *Seeing Like a State* nine times, and more of Scott's work besides. He's clearly part of the canon here.

The music feels personal in a way the books don't. Van Morrison, Joni Mitchell, Springsteen: it sounds like Amit's own record collection, not a list made for the audience. That makes it more interesting, not less.

The articles show what the show is really built on. Economics leads, with philosophy and Indian policy close behind. Amid the policy papers there are poems, literary essays and cricket pieces. The show refuses to be one thing, and so does its reading list.
